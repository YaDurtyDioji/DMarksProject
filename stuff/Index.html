Framework Overview

The textbook (Gido et al.) organises practical teaching around the project life cycle. The PMBOK® 7 presents a broader system for delivering value through principles and eight interacting performance domains.

The 12 Project Management Principles (PMBOK 7)

PrincipleMeaning in practice
1. StewardshipAct responsibly, ethically and with care for the project, people, resources and wider impacts.
2. TeamCreate a collaborative environment in which people can contribute and work effectively.
3. StakeholdersEngage stakeholders proactively and constructively; understand their needs and influence.
4. ValueFocus on the intended benefits and usefulness of the project, not merely producing outputs.
5. Systems thinkingRecognise that project components interact; a change in one area can affect the whole.
6. LeadershipDemonstrate leadership behaviours appropriate to the people and situation.
7. TailoringAdapt the approach, governance, methods and controls to the project's context.
8. QualityBuild quality into processes and deliverables rather than relying only on final inspection.
9. ComplexityNavigate complexity by understanding its sources and responding appropriately.
10. RiskOptimise risk responses by considering threats and opportunities.
11. Adaptability and resiliencyRemain able to adjust to change and recover from setbacks.
12. ChangeEnable the transition from the current state to the envisioned future state.

The 8 Project Performance Domains (PMBOK 7)

StakeholdersBuild productive relationships and engagement with people or groups affected by, or able to affect, the project.
TeamEstablish a high-performing team through leadership, collaboration, development and a healthy team culture.
Development approach and life cycleChoose and adapt predictive, adaptive or hybrid approaches, delivery cadence and project phases.
PlanningOrganise and coordinate project work, estimates, resources, communications, procurement, changes and measures.
Project workCoordinate the people and processes that perform the work, manage resources and procurement, and handle changes.
DeliveryProduce outputs that meet requirements and enable intended outcomes and value.

Remember the distinction: Principles guide how people behave. Performance domains describe broad areas of project work that must be managed together.

LU1 - Introduction to Project Management

1. What is a project?

A project is a temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service or result. It has a defined purpose and an identifiable beginning and end. A project is different from ongoing operations, which are repetitive activities that sustain the organisation.

ProjectOperation
Build and launch a new online booking platform.Process bookings every day using the live platform.
Open a new restaurant branch.Prepare and serve meals at an established branch.
Implement a new network at a campus.Provide routine network support and maintenance.
Memory hook: A project changes or creates something; operations keep the existing business running.

2. Common project attributes

  • Clear objective: A defined result or outcome that explains what the project is intended to achieve.
  • Interdependent activities: Activities are linked by logical or technical dependencies; some must precede others.
  • Resources: People, skills, equipment, materials, facilities, information and money are required.
  • Time frame: The work is temporary, with a start and an intended completion point.
  • Uniqueness: The combination of context, stakeholders, requirements and constraints makes the endeavour distinct.
  • Sponsor/customer: A sponsor provides direction or authorisation; a customer or end user receives or uses the result.
  • Uncertainty: Estimates, assumptions and conditions may change; threats and opportunities must be managed.

3. Balancing project constraints

The textbook highlights scope, quality, schedule, budget, resources, risk and customer satisfaction as constraints that need to be balanced. Scope, time and cost are often taught as the traditional 'triple constraint', but they are not the only constraints. A change to one variable can affect several others.

Example: adding two extra features to a booking system may increase development time, testing effort, cost and delivery risk.

Exam-ready explanation: Project constraints compete. The project manager must make trade-offs while protecting quality, stakeholder expectations and the intended project outcome.

4. Project life cycle

Textbook phaseMain purposeTypical work / evidence
InitiatingRecognise the need and authorise the project.Identify need; evaluate/choose project; appoint project manager; prepare or approve the project charter.
PlanningDecide how the objective will be achieved.Define scope; develop WBS; assign responsibility; sequence and estimate activities; prepare schedule, budget and risk plans.
PerformingCarry out the planned work and manage delivery.Coordinate people and resources; produce deliverables; communicate; monitor progress and manage approved changes.
ClosingFormally complete or terminate the work.Confirm acceptance; make final payments; evaluate performance; capture lessons learned; archive records and release resources.

Note: Monitoring and controlling is not merely a single isolated phase: the textbook discusses monitoring progress and controlling changes as work is performed.

5. Project management and value

Project management involves applying knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project work to meet requirements and achieve intended outcomes. PMBOK® 7 places particular emphasis on value: completing a deliverable is not enough if the deliverable does not produce the benefit that justified the project.

Example: a new student portal is an output. Faster registration, fewer administrative errors and improved student experience are intended outcomes.

6. Stakeholders

A stakeholder is an individual, group or organisation that can affect, be affected by, or perceive itself to be affected by a project. Identify stakeholders early, understand their expectations and influence, and maintain appropriate two-way communication.

Exam tip: When asked to explain a project life cycle, name each phase, state its purpose and give one or two typical activities. Add that monitoring, communication and change management continue during delivery.

LU2 - Identifying and Selecting Projects

1. Why organisations select projects

Organisations have limited funding, time, skills and capacity. Project selection helps them choose work that best supports strategic objectives and offers an acceptable balance of benefits, costs, risks and resource demands.

  • Strategic alignment: does the project support organisational goals?
  • Expected benefits and value: what financial or non-financial improvement is expected?
  • Cost and affordability: can the organisation fund the work and its ongoing consequences?
  • Risk and feasibility: are the technical, legal, operational and commercial uncertainties acceptable?
  • Resource availability: are the necessary people, skills and equipment available?
  • Timing and dependencies: is the project needed now, and does it depend on other work?

PMBOK® 7 links project justification to the business need, cost-benefit reasoning and business strategy. The justification should be revisited if circumstances change or the project is no longer expected to provide value.

2. Project charter

A project charter is a high-level authorising document. It records the project purpose, objectives, funding, major deliverables, milestones, assumptions, constraints, risks and approval requirements.

Typical charter elementWhat it answers
Project titleWhat is the project called?
Purpose / business needWhy is the project being undertaken?
High-level descriptionWhat broad work or solution is envisaged?
ObjectiveWhat measurable result, by when and within what budget?
Success criteriaHow will success be judged?
Funding / budget authorityWhat funding is authorised?
Major deliverablesWhat significant outputs must be produced?
Acceptance criteriaWhat standards must the outputs meet to be accepted?
Milestone scheduleWhat are the key dates or decision points?
AssumptionsWhat conditions are being treated as true for planning?
ConstraintsWhat limits must the project respect?
Major risksWhat significant uncertainties are already known?
Approval / authorityWho authorises the project and accepts accountability?
Memory hook: The charter is the project's authorisation and high-level direction—not the detailed day-to-day project plan.

3. Request for Proposal (RFP)

An RFP is a formal request issued to potential suppliers or contractors. It describes the customer's need and asks respondents to propose a solution, explain how they would perform the work, identify relevant capability and provide schedule and pricing information.

A clear RFP describes the required outcome, scope, specifications, deliverables and acceptance expectations. Ambiguous requirements create a risk of inconsistent bids, misunderstandings, disputes and later change requests.

4. Project charter versus RFP

Project charterRFP
Authorises and frames a project within the sponsoring organisation.Invites external suppliers/contractors to propose how they would meet a customer's need.
Usually prepared or approved by the sponsor/initiating organisation.Issued by a customer or procuring organisation to potential providers.
Focuses on why the project exists, its high-level objective, authority and boundaries.Focuses on what the customer needs and what bidders must submit.
Exam tip: A charter authorises the project. An RFP solicits proposals from potential providers. Do not describe them as interchangeable documents.

LU3 - Developing Project Proposals

1. Bid / no-bid decision

A contractor should assess whether responding to an RFP is commercially and operationally sensible before investing effort in a proposal.

  • Strategic fit: does the opportunity align with the contractor's business and expertise?
  • Customer and funding: is the customer credible, committed and able to fund the project?
  • Resources and capability: can the contractor provide the required people, skills, technology and capacity?
  • Risk: are the technical, schedule, legal, financial and contractual risks manageable?
  • Competition: can the contractor offer a credible and differentiated solution?
  • Probability of success and cost of bidding: is the potential return worth the proposal effort?

2. Proposal contents

A proposal is a persuasive response to the customer's requirements. It must show that the contractor understands the need, can deliver the work and has priced it credibly.

SectionWhat it should demonstrate
TechnicalUnderstanding of the customer's problem; proposed solution; scope and deliverables; technical approach; how requirements and acceptance criteria will be met.
ManagementProject organisation; named roles and responsibilities; relevant experience; schedule and milestones; resources; communication, quality and risk arrangements.
CostPrice and cost basis; assumptions; resource or cost breakdown where appropriate; payment approach; exclusions and other pricing conditions.

3. Contracts

A contract is an agreement between the customer and contractor. It should make the deliverables, specifications, responsibilities, price or payment basis, schedule, acceptance requirements and relevant terms clear.

FeatureFixed-price contractCost-reimbursement contract
Payment basisAgreed price for the defined work; changes require agreement.Customer pays allowable actual costs plus an agreed fee/profit.
Customer riskGenerally lower cost risk if scope is stable and clearly specified.Generally higher: actual costs can exceed the original estimate.
Contractor riskGenerally higher: overruns can reduce profit or create a loss.Generally lower for reimbursable costs, subject to contract terms and performance obligations.
Best fitWell-defined work with relatively low uncertainty.Work with substantial uncertainty, where costs cannot be estimated reliably upfront.
ExampleProducing a brochure to detailed specifications.Research and development where the amount of testing is uncertain.
Exam memory hook: Fixed price: the contractor carries more cost-overrun risk. Cost reimbursement: the customer carries more cost-overrun risk.

Important qualification: actual risk depends on the contract wording, scope clarity, change provisions, incentives, caps and governance.

4. Proposal quality

Respond directly to the RFP and make compliance easy to verify. Explain the solution rather than merely repeating the customer's wording. Use realistic estimates and state assumptions and exclusions. Show that the proposed team, schedule and resources are credible.

LU4 - Defining Scope, Quality, Responsibility and Sequence

1. Project objective and SMART thinking

The project objective states the result the project is expected to accomplish. It should be clear enough that the customer and project team share the same understanding of success. SMART is a useful memory aid: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant/Realistic and Time-bound.

Weak objectiveStronger objective
Build a website.Launch a 10-page e-commerce website with an agreed payment gateway, user acceptance testing and handover by 1 December, within the approved R150,000 budget.

2. Project scope and scope creep

Project scope describes the work that must be performed to produce the agreed deliverables and meet the acceptance criteria.

  • In scope: explicitly included features, services, work and deliverables.
  • Out of scope: work or features that are not included in the approved project.
  • Acceptance criteria: measurable or testable conditions used to determine whether a deliverable is acceptable.
  • Scope creep: uncontrolled expansion of work, often through small additions that are not assessed or formally approved.

How to control scope creep: Record the request, assess its effect on scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources and risk, obtain the appropriate approval, then update the relevant plans and communicate the decision.

3. Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

A WBS is a hierarchical decomposition of the total project scope into manageable components, organised around deliverables or work.

Memory hook: Break a large pizza into slices, then divide each slice into manageable portions. The smallest planned WBS component is commonly called a work package.
LevelExample: Campus booking system
ProjectCampus booking system
Major deliverableBooking website
Sub-deliverableBooking interface
Work packageApproved booking form design and specification

Important distinction: A WBS is not simply a chronological to-do list. It organises the scope into components/deliverables. Activities describe the work needed to create those components.

4. Responsibility Assignment Matrix (RAM)

A RAM links WBS work packages or activities to the people or roles responsible for performing or supporting them.

Work packageProject managerDeveloperTesterCustomer rep
Requirements approvalSSSP
Build booking formSPS—
Test booking flowSSPS
Accept final releaseSSSP

P = Primary responsibility; S = Support. The study-note convention is one clearly accountable primary owner for each work package. In practice, a RACI matrix may be used instead (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed).

5. Quality planning

Quality planning identifies the standards and acceptance requirements that deliverables and work processes must satisfy. Quality should be built into how work is performed, not left until the final inspection.

6. Activities, dependencies and network diagrams

Activities are the work tasks required to produce project deliverables. A network diagram represents the logical sequence and dependencies between activities.

  • Finish-to-start dependency: the successor activity cannot start until its predecessor finishes.
  • Parallel activities: tasks that can proceed at the same time because they do not depend on one another.
  • Laddering: a scheduling technique for repetitive work where a following activity can begin on an earlier completed portion before the preceding activity has finished everywhere.
Exam tip: Explain the relationship between WBS, RAM and network diagram: WBS breaks down scope; RAM assigns ownership; network diagram shows activity sequence and dependencies.

LU5 - Project Management Information Systems (PMIS)

1. What is a PMIS?

A project management information system is a collection of tools and information used to plan, organise, monitor and control project work. Examples include Microsoft Project, Jira, Asana and other scheduling, collaboration or portfolio tools.

  • Store activities, durations, dependencies, resources and cost information.
  • Generate schedules, Gantt charts, network views and reports.
  • Compare actual performance with baselines and highlight variances.
  • Support resource allocation, forecasting and what-if analysis.
  • Improve visibility and communication among project participants.

2. Why project software is useful

Project software can automate calculations and help the project manager understand the consequences of changes. For example, changing an activity duration may cause dependent dates, slack and the critical path to be recalculated.

Technical accuracy: It is safer to say that Microsoft Project stores linked project data and automatically recalculates schedule information when relevant inputs change.

3. Advantages and limitations

AdvantagesLimitations / concerns
Fast recalculation and reporting.Incorrect input produces misleading output (garbage in, garbage out).
Improved visibility of dates, dependencies and resources.A polished chart can create false confidence if assumptions are weak.
Supports baseline-versus-actual comparisons.Software cannot replace judgement, communication or leadership.
Helps model schedule and resource scenarios.Complex tools require training, configuration and maintenance.
Provides a shared source of project information.Poor update discipline makes reports stale or unreliable.

4. PMIS and PMBOK® 7

PMBOK® 7 does not prescribe a particular software package. It emphasises selecting methods and artefacts that suit the project. A Gantt chart, schedule, dashboard, risk register or backlog is useful only when it helps the team make decisions and achieve the intended outcomes.

LU6 - Developing the Schedule

1. Build a credible schedule

A schedule is developed from defined activities, logical dependencies, resource needs and duration estimates. It should be updated as actual performance and new information become available. The schedule is a model of the plan, not a guarantee that the future will unfold exactly as predicted.

2. Forward pass: earliest times

TermMeaning / rule
ES – Earliest StartEarliest time an activity can begin after its predecessors are complete.
EF – Earliest FinishEF = ES + Duration
Forward passMove from the start of the network to the finish. Where there are multiple predecessors, use the latest predecessor EF as the successor ES.

3. Backward pass: latest times

TermMeaning / rule
LF – Latest FinishLatest time an activity can finish without delaying the project completion date.
LS – Latest StartLS = LF – Duration
Backward passMove from the end of the network towards the start. Where there are multiple successors, use the earliest successor LS as the predecessor LF.

4. Total slack (float)

Total slack is the amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying the planned project completion date, assuming the network logic and project finish constraint remain unchanged.

Total Slack = LF - EF, or Total Slack = LS - ES

5. Critical path

The critical path is the longest-duration path through the activity network and determines the shortest possible project duration for the stated logic and estimates. Activities on a critical path have zero total slack in a standard unconstrained critical-path calculation. A delay to a critical activity can delay the project finish unless corrective action is taken.

Memory hook: The project cannot finish until its longest chain of dependent activities is complete. That longest chain is the critical path.

6. Mini worked example

Assume a simple network: A (3 days) starts first. B (4 days) and C (2 days) both depend on A. D (3 days) depends on both B and C.

ActivityDurationPredecessorESEFLSLFSlack
A3—03030
B4A37370
C2A35572
D3B, C7107100

Forward pass: A finishes at day 3. B finishes at day 7; C finishes at day 5. D cannot start until both predecessors are complete, so D starts at day 7 and finishes at day 10.
Backward pass: gives C two days of slack.
Critical path: A → B → D.
Project duration: 10 days.

7. Resource scheduling

TechniqueWhat stays fixed?What can change?Purpose
Resource leveling / smoothingRequired project completion time (deadline)Resource use / timing of activitiesReduce peaks and fluctuations in resource demand without extending the required finish date.
Resource-limited schedulingAvailable resource quantitiesProject completion time may extendCreate the shortest feasible schedule without exceeding fixed resource limits.

8. Schedule control

  • Compare actual dates and progress with the approved schedule baseline.
  • Identify the cause and effect of variances.
  • Assess proposed changes and their impact on dependencies, resources, cost and risk.
  • Agree corrective action, update the schedule where authorised and communicate the change.
  • Reforecast completion and monitor whether the corrective action works.

LU7 - Determining Costs, Budget and Earned Value

1. Budget terminology

TermMeaning
Total Budgeted Cost (TBC)The sum of the estimated/budgeted costs assigned to the project's work packages or activities.
Cumulative Budgeted Cost (CBC)The time-phased cumulative budget: how much should have been budgeted/spent by a given point in the schedule. It is comparable to planned value (PV).
Cumulative Actual Cost (CAC)The cumulative actual amount spent to perform work up to the status date; comparable to actual cost (AC).
Cumulative Earned Value (CEV)The budgeted value of the work actually completed by the status date; comparable to earned value (EV).

Earned value compares the budgeted value of completed work with the actual cost of that work. It is more informative than looking at spending alone.

2. Core earned-value formulas

MeasureFormulaInterpretation
Cost Performance Index (CPI)CPI = CEV ÷ CAC (EV ÷ AC)Efficiency of cost use. Below 1.0 = over budget for the work performed; above 1.0 = under budget for the work performed.
Cost Variance (CV)CV = CEV - CAC (EV - AC)Negative = over budget; positive = under budget; zero = exactly on budget for work performed.
Forecast cost at completion (FCAC / EAC)FCAC = TBC ÷ CPIForecast if current cost efficiency continues for the remaining work.

3. Worked example

Suppose the budgeted value of completed work (CEV) is R80,000, actual cost (CAC) is R100,000 and the total budget (TBC) is R200,000.

CPI = R80,000 ÷ R100,000 = 0.80
CV = R80,000 - R100,000 = -R20,000
FCAC = R200,000 ÷ 0.80 = R250,000

Interpretation: The project earns R0.80 of budgeted value for every R1.00 spent. It is currently over budget for the work completed. If the same cost efficiency continues, the forecast final cost is R250,000, which is R50,000 above the original R200,000 budget.

Exam method: Write the formula → substitute the figures → calculate → state what the result means. Never leave a CPI or CV answer uninterpreted.

4. Common errors to avoid

  • Do not confuse actual cost with the budgeted value of work completed.
  • A project can spend less than planned simply because work is behind schedule; low spending alone does not prove good cost performance.
  • CPI below 1.0 means poor cost efficiency; it does not mean the project is 0.8% over budget.
  • The FCAC/EAC formula assumes current cost performance is a reasonable forecast for the remaining work. Other forecasting assumptions may produce different estimates.

LU8 - Managing Risk

1. What is project risk?

A project risk is an uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, can affect one or more project objectives. A negative risk is a threat; a positive risk is an opportunity. Risk management is not limited to preventing problems—it also includes identifying and increasing beneficial opportunities.

2. Risk management process

StepWhat the team doesExample
1. IdentifyIdentify possible sources/events that could affect objectives. Consider technical, schedule, cost, people, procurement, legal and external factors.A key developer may leave before system testing.
2. AssessEstimate likelihood and impact; prioritise risks that deserve attention.High likelihood and high impact.
3. Plan responsesChoose a suitable strategy, owner, action and trigger/contingency where appropriate.Cross-train another developer and document critical components.
4. MonitorTrack known risks, identify new risks, check triggers and assess whether responses work.Review staff availability at each project checkpoint.

3. Risk assessment matrix

A risk matrix combines likelihood (how probable the event is) and impact (how serious the effect would be).

Likelihood \ ImpactLow impactMedium impactHigh impact
High likelihoodMediumHighVery high
Medium likelihoodLow/mediumMediumHigh
Low likelihoodLowLow/mediumMedium

4. Threat response strategies

StrategyMeaningExample
AvoidChange the plan or conditions so the threat is eliminated or bypassed.Move the installation away from a known flood zone.
MitigateReduce the likelihood and/or impact of the threat.Back up data and test restoration to reduce the impact of data loss.
TransferShift financial or other consequences to a third party, usually through an agreement.Insurance or a suitable contractual allocation of risk.
AcceptAcknowledge the risk and decide not to take proactive action beyond monitoring or a contingency, as appropriate.Accept a low-impact delay risk and monitor it.

For opportunities, common response options include exploiting, enhancing, sharing or accepting the opportunity.

Avoid versus mitigate: Avoid = change the plan so the threat no longer applies. Mitigate = keep the activity or exposure but reduce the chance of the event and/or the damage it could cause.

5. Risk register and ongoing monitoring

A risk register (called a risk assessment matrix in the textbook's terminology) can record the risk description, cause, likelihood, impact, priority, response, owner, trigger and status. Risk management is continuous because new information and changing conditions can alter the risk profile.

LU9 - The Project Manager and Closing the Project

1. Project manager responsibilities

ResponsibilityWhat it involves
PlanningEstablish objectives and scope; coordinate estimates, schedule, resources, budget and risk responses.
OrganisingArrange people, roles, resources, communication and work structures.
Monitoring and controllingCompare actual performance with plans; identify variances; manage issues and approved changes.
LeadingBuild commitment, resolve conflict, support the team, communicate and guide decisions.

2. Project manager skills

  • Leadership: provide direction, motivate people and adapt leadership style to the situation.
  • Communication: explain objectives, listen, share information and manage expectations.
  • Interpersonal skills: build trust, collaborate and handle relationships constructively.
  • Problem-solving: define issues, analyse causes, generate options and implement solutions.
  • Negotiation: reach workable agreements with customers, suppliers and team members.
  • Stress and time management: prioritise, remain composed and use time effectively.
  • Developing people and delegation: match work to capability, provide support and grow team competence.
Memory hook: The project manager is like a coach: the coach does not perform every task, but creates the conditions, direction and coordination that help the team perform.

3. Closing the project

Closing is a planned part of project management, not simply the moment the team stops working. The textbook identifies five key closing actions.

Closing actionWhat to doWhy it matters
1. Final paymentsCollect amounts due; pay suppliers, subcontractors and other parties; close accounts.Completes financial obligations and enables final cost review.
2. Staff recognition and evaluationRecognise contributions and evaluate performance.Acknowledges effort and supports learning and team morale.
3. Post-project evaluationReview the project's results, performance and customer feedback.Determines what worked and what did not.
4. Lessons learnedDocument useful knowledge, decisions, problems and recommendations.Improves future projects and prevents repeated mistakes.
5. Archive project documentsOrganise and retain relevant records, approvals, plans, changes and deliverables.Supports future reference, audits, legal needs and organisational learning.
Exam favourite: Lessons learned are captured so that knowledge gained during the project can be reused. They should be documented and communicated—not left as informal memories.

4. Early project termination

A project may be terminated before completion if its business need disappears, strategic priorities change, funding is withdrawn, costs or delays become unacceptable, serious risks emerge, or the customer no longer wants the outcome. PMBOK® 7 similarly notes that a project may be ended when it is no longer aligned with the business need or is unlikely to deliver intended value.

5. Formal acceptance and transition

Before closing, confirm that agreed deliverables have been provided and meet the acceptance criteria. Depending on the project, this may include customer sign-off, handover, training, operational support arrangements, contract closure and release of project resources.

6. Leadership in PMBOK® 7

PMBOK® 7 treats leadership as a behaviour that can be demonstrated by different people, not merely as authority attached to a job title. The project manager should foster collaboration, engage stakeholders, tailor leadership style and help the team respond to change.

A Reliable Structure for Written Exam Answers

  1. Define the concept accurately in one or two sentences.
  2. Explain its purpose: why does a project manager use it?
  3. Describe the main elements, steps or categories.
  4. Apply it to the scenario with a concrete example.
  5. State the implication or recommendation, including effects on scope, time, cost, quality, resources, risk or stakeholders where relevant.
Final study advice: Prioritise understanding the logic of the tools. For a calculation, show every step. For a theory question, define, explain, apply and conclude.
